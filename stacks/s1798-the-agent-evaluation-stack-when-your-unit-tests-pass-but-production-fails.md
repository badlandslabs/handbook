# S-1798 · The Agent Evaluation Stack — When Your Unit Tests Pass But Production Fails

Your agent demoed perfectly. The user asked exactly the right questions, the API stayed up, the model cooperated. You shipped it. Two weeks later a customer hit an edge case, the agent hallucinated a refund amount, and nobody noticed for three days because nothing broke — nothing logged an error, nothing failed a test. The agent just produced a wrong answer confidently and moved on.

You need evaluation infrastructure before the next deploy.

## Forces

- **Endpoint scoring is necessary but not sufficient.** An agent can reach the right final answer via the wrong path: call the wrong tool first, ignore a policy constraint, and get lucky because the recovery happened to work out. Endpoint accuracy certifies answers, not behaviour. The failure mode that burns in production lives in the trajectory, not the output.
- **Benchmarks overestimate real-world reliability.** Popular evaluation benchmarks (SWE-bench, AgentBench, τ-bench) test whether an agent *can* complete a task — not how consistently or robustly it does so under operational stress. SWE-bench's top score on Level 3 GAIA tasks is 61%; the best agent still fails 39% of difficult tasks. A 90% benchmark score is not a 90% production success rate.
- **The feedback loop is broken by default.** When you change a prompt, swap a model, or adjust retrieval logic, most teams cannot answer whether they made things better or worse. The broken feedback loop in AI development turns engineering into guesswork. Over 40% of AI agent projects are predicted to fail by 2027 (Gartner), and quality issues are the top cited barrier.
- **Trajectory failures compound.** In multi-step agents, a wrong tool call in step 3 propagates garbage into step 4. Traditional observability (logs, traces, dashboards) tells you what happened; evaluation tells you whether what happened was right. You need both, and most teams only have the former.

## The Move

Build a layered evaluation harness that scores agents at the trajectory level — not just the final answer. The minimum viable setup:

- **Golden dataset first.** Curate 50–200 real examples from production traces and user interactions, not synthetic queries. Each example should include the query, expected correct answer or outcome, category (factual/eligibility/procedural/comparative/temporal), and difficulty rating. Without ground truth, your evals are measuring vibes.
- **Score the trajectory, not the endpoint.** Evaluate each tool call in sequence: was the right tool called, with the right parameters, and was the response handled correctly? An agent that reaches the right answer through a reckless path is a production liability. Use step-level rubrics that encode your policies — what tools are allowed in what contexts, what constraints must not be violated.
- **Run statistical suites, not single runs.** Execute each example 10+ times across model versions and temperature settings. Single-run evals hide variance. Track pass rate, consistency (does it succeed on the same task across runs?), and robustness (does it handle inputs slightly off from the golden examples?).
- **Use LLM-as-judge for nuanced qualities, not as your only scorer.** Over 57% of surveyed production agent teams rely on judge LLMs for quality gating. Smaller distilled judges achieve ~97% cost reduction versus full model judges while maintaining sufficient accuracy for most use cases. Combine with code-based deterministic checks for factual correctness, tool call accuracy, and safety assertions. One practitioner documented: "LLMs are notoriously bad at handling numeric ranges," which matters if you're scoring on specific values.
- **Run replay harnesses against production captures.** Capture real traces from production, then re-run them against new model versions or updated policies without re-hitting live systems. This lets you regression-test on actual failure cases. Production traces become your most valuable test cases.
- **Wire evals into CI as a gate.** Treat eval failures like test failures: block deploys below your quality threshold. The eval suite is the contract between your agent's behaviour and your users' expectations.
- **Distinguish evaluation from observability.** Evaluation scores outputs *before* deployment. Observability monitors behaviour *during* production. The most effective teams use the same scorer and rubric across both stages — so what fails in eval will be caught in prod, not surprised.

## Gotchas

- **Benchmarks ≠ production reliability.** A 95% SWE-bench score does not mean 95% of your customers will have a good experience. Use benchmarks for directional signal and capability pre-screening, not release gates.
- **LLM-as-judge has known biases.** Position bias (preferring the first or last answer in comparisons), verbosity bias (rewarding longer responses), and poor numeric discrimination all documented in production. Validate your judge against human ratings before relying on it for high-stakes decisions.
- **Golden datasets go stale.** User input distributions drift. Questions change as products evolve. Your golden dataset is a snapshot — re-annotate and expand it quarterly, or your evals will stop reflecting reality.
- **Single-run eval gives false confidence.** Agent behaviour varies across identical runs due to non-determinism. Without multiple runs per example, you don't know whether a pass represents consistent competence or luck.
- **Trajectory scoring is harder than it looks.** Writing per-step rubrics that capture "did the agent do the right thing at this step, given everything it knew so far?" requires domain expertise and policy clarity. Teams often underestimate the work of defining the rubric itself.
- **Eval framework lock-in is real.** Promptfoo (acquired by OpenAI, March 2026), LangSmith (cloud-hosted), and DeepEval (open-source, actively maintained) each have different APIs, scorer formats, and CI integrations. Choose based on your team'sops model, not just feature parity.
